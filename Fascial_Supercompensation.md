# FASCIAL SUPERCOMPENSATION

## INTRO AND THEORY OF OPERATION

For most of modern medical history, the fascial system was regarded as largely passive stabilization for muscular mediated performance. Whether anaerobic max effort lifts, steady aerobic capacity, or fast powerful movements, the fascia was believed to play a secondary, supporting role to muscle tissue. However, this understanding has begun to change over the past decade, if not slowly (by sports science and coaches).

It is now understood that the fascial system is in fact a primary, first class citizen that needs specific consideration in training plans, regardless of the sport or level. Containing more neurons than muscles it is not only a sensory organ; but it provides static tension/stability during mechanical tension (lifting); stretch-shortening reflex for energy preservation during certain aerobic loads; stretch-shortning reflex for plyometric speed/response; viscoelastic shear thickening for impact resistance; and aligned collagen fibers for strong end ROM stability and strength.

Training these fascial qualities require specific stress loading to achieve an adaptation response; and crucially, must be combined with recovery modalities for optimal progress. While these modalities have significant overlap with muscular recovery; the lack of capillaries (blood flow), and the unique nutrient requirements demand a modified recovery stack.

This writeup explores the principles of optimal stress/recovery and various modalities (the stack), such that a realistic plan can be adapted for anyone at any level.

## FASCIAL NUTRITION AND SUPPLEMENTATION

Fascial nutrition, while not complex in practice, is perhaps the most under-served aspect of the average gym-goer nutritional profile. **Timing**:  The supplements below should be taken 30-60 minutes before fascial work begins. This makes them available in the blood stream at approximately the moment that mechanical stimulation can drive and transport them into the capillary-poor fascial tissues - more on mechanical transport in the next section. These are listed in rough order of importance.

- Collagen. Fascia is made of collagen, which is the raw construction material. While muscles have a somewhat flexible window of protein intake/recovery timing, there is much less flexibility for collagen consumption and fascia. It must be taken *before* physical activity, absorbed into the blood stream, and available for mechanical transport into fascial tissue via physical movement. This means pre-workout.

- Vitamin C. If collagen is the raw material, vitamin C is a worker. It drives hydroxylation which lock collagen strands into a stable triple helix structure. As with most nutrients, natural sources are superior to artificial ones.

- Copper. It cross-links the formed collagen helix into a strong resilient matrix. Most people don't get enough Cu in their diets already.

- Hyaluronic Acid. Lubricant. A hydrophilic (water absorbing) gel-like compound that maintains hydration of fascial sheets, for low friction gliding past each other. The incompressible hydraulic properties are also fundamental to impact shock absorption and distribution. 

*[The above are essential. Take them all with water, salt, and at least some carbs]*
*[The below are important, but play moreso supporting roles]*

- Boron. More of a supporting role that lowers SHGB (which increases free testosterone), reduces inflammation, helps with bone health, and fascial anchor points to the bone. It can also be taken with the stack above.

- Vitamin D, sunlight, infrared. These all generally go together, and are also play a supporting role in collagen formation. Minimum dose might be considered 10-20 minutes in 6-8 UV index sunlight.

- Electrolytes. Sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphorous. This isnt an exploration on optimal mineral balance, but suffice it to say, if you're not thinking about minerals and electrolytes and doing basic calculations, odds are you're deficient in at least one of these.

- Egg yolks. Again, more supplementary support than direct fascial construction, but there are many important nutrients that egg yolks provide. For the gym-bros of the world, this is also a good way to get additional pre-workout protein.

*[Everything above can be taken together as a pre-workout stack]*

- Serrapeptase. This enzyme disassembles scar tissue, biofilm, fibrin. Poorly formed, poorly aligned, misplaced/deposited collagen. Despite being farther down on the list, this one is highly recommended. ***Should be taken on an empty stomach, and don't eat for the next 30 minutes after.***

## NUTRIENT DELIVERY

Fascia relies on a combination of mechanical force/squeezing, shearing (fascial sheets sliding over one another), heat (thixotropy - viscosity reduction), imbibition (like a sponge re-absorbing after mechanical squeezing), and special cells which open ion channels (fibroblast mechanotransduction). While your muscles themselves can recover with nutrition and rest, optimal fascial recovery depends on active processes intricately linked to the lymphatic system.

On workout days, a good pre-workout routine will activate these mechanisms prior to full loading. Done efficiently, this can be accomplished in about 20-30 minutes. Done fully/completely, might require up to an hour to truly target all tissues/systems. This can be done with any time of day that fits your schedule. In general, most full body workout routines will deliver nutrients to most of your fasical system.

On recovery days (the day following acute stress), minor/gentle **non**-microtrauma loads must be applied, to remove accumulated waste products and deliver fresh nutrients. Think of squeezing a dirty sponge and absorbing fresh clean water. The ideal timing is late morning, where you can step outside, in the sunlight, and perform a 20-30 minute gentle loading scheme that removes the waste products and stiffeness accumulated from the night before; and delivers the nutrient stack that you consumed 45-60 minutes prior.

*A note on timing: On an empty stomach, 30 minutes might be enough to absorb the collagen peptides, whereas taken with a full meal, might require 90 minutes before they're readily available in the blood stream.*

Below are various means of inducing nutrient delivery, without significant loading or microtrauma. Research suggests a 6-8 hour window afterwards of increased collagen protein synthesis.

### Heat/sauna
Increases body temperature which melts adhesions and reduces the viscocity of the hyaluronic gel between your fascial sheaths, and opens receptivity for nutrients. It doesnt matter that much how hot or how long, it only matters if you increased your body temperature. This can be estimated by heart rate, targetting ~115-130 bpm. You should try to get some gentle movement if possible. Hips, spine, shoulders, neck. It should only take 15-20 minutes to get your heart rate up in a 60-70 degC sauna. From here you can go directly into an outdoor session.

### Neurogenic Tremors (shaking)
Rapid oscillations which require only moderate levels of tension/flex. It might even look like you're convulsing, and it might might look or feel a bit autistic (especially when you realize how good it feels). This activates numerous mechanisms at once. Mechanical tension (squeezing the sponge), fascial sheet shearing, and neurological modulation (gamma loop reset, **pandiculation**, muscle spindle tension). Shaking is a little known tool for releasing stiffness, tension, kinks, and for rapid warmup even when cold.  

It might even be a good idea to do a 10-20 second full body pandiculation after sitting for a long period of time, to loosen everything and continue nutrient turnover in the system. Again, the point is not loading, but light exploration. You can try various positions/movements of shoulders, hips, arms, legs, ankles, neck, and from various ranges of motion. Nothing extreme. A vibration plate can offer some of the benefits, but you'll miss out on some of the internally generated mechanical pump benefits.

### Ischemic Compression (trigger point / tissue smashing)
A mechanism for fascial surface shearing, tissue compression (squeezing the sponge), and neurological adaptation. Often times a fascial tissue or a specific location can be too dehydrated, too many adhesions have formed between sliding surfaces, and too much neurological guarding has taken place as a result, that the waste/nutrient turnover required to restore function cannot be accomplished with ordinary dynamic movements (the other modalities listed here). 

Ischemic compression and sheering, force the issue at site specific locations. However, because you can only focus on one spot at a time, it is a slower means of achieving nutrient turnover along a major fiber/group. Thus it is recommended for your worst problem points, or potentially a few focused sessions that address the full length of problematic muscles. Deep tissue sports massage can help you get to places that might be hard to reach.

### Stretching
This term "stretching" can mean two different things. In this context of recovery, we're talking about gentle dynamic stretching. You might slowly rock in/out of a stretched position over the course of 30-60 seconds, while "exploring" various angles and body positions at that end range. Primarily you're coaxing neurological relaxation (parasympathetic expression), and mechanical action to activate waste/nutrient turnover.

### Gentle Flex/Release
The act of flexing a muscle brings blood/nutrients into the muscle, activates the lymphatic pump (clearing waste), and slightly loads the fascial tissue well below microtrauma levels.

## FASCIAL ADAPTATION

Traditionally fascial adaptation is conceptualized as: inflammation, proliferation, remodeling. This is generally correct, but in reality, we should also include a neurological component, and realize that proliferation, remodeling, and neurological organization will all happen in tandem in a well designed training system.

Very important: Inflammation is not something to fear or quash. Inflammation is necessary as an acute response, waste removal, nutrient delivery; and a sensory mechanism as to the state of the system. If you kill the inflammation (with ice, or nsaids, or even steroids), you greatly hamper the recovery. Instead, we manage inflammation, work with it, and encourage the system to relax by providing what it needs. If we manage the load and recovery correctly, inflammation should be near baseline within 24-48 hours of a loaded session.

Below are the primary fascial adaptations and supercompensations that we seek to induce with controlled session loads:

### Neurological (parasympathetic engagement)
Neurological comfort and release are actually an early primary improvement for sedentary restoration, or from an "old injury that never fully healed." The neurological system must be gently coaxed into accepting and integrating new (or lost) capacity. The initial gains can happen on a timeline of days-to-weeks. Starting out, you'll typically see rapid advancement on this front.

### Adhesion removal
Fascial surfaces which remain stagnant for months/years can form strong adhesions that must be broken to restore gliding capacity. The most sticky areas should typically start with trigger point therapy to restore basic function. This can happen in basically just a handful of sessions. It's important to not get stuck here (trigger pointing forever).

### Hydration
The fascial system is a wet system, a hydraulic system. Adaptation implies fuller gel and sliding surfaces. Healthy water weight.

### Collagen Remodeling
Alignment and cross-linking of existing collagen fibers. Untrained collagen/fascia are disorderly. Proper loading will, over time, cause these fibers to align along the direction of forces applied, making an equivalent amount of tissue, significantly stronger. Cross-linkage of these fibers will also happen here, as well as replacement of type III (scar tissue) with type I (strong organized collagen matrix). This happens on at timeline of months.

### Hysterisis Improvement
Hysteresis is the energy lost as heat during the "springy" loading/unloading in quick/rebound movements. Well formed fascia has a "crimped" macrostructure which provides for more efficient spring loading of the tissue, where less heat is lost during the "spring" of a stretch-shortening cycle. 

### Longitudinal Deposition 
Increase in the actual fiber length. While initial ROM gains typically derive from neurological relaxation; eventually new range must be found in new growth of collagen fibers, which requires proliferation. The timeline on this is months.

### Radial Epimysium Growth
The epimysium is the "sheath" which surrounds and contains muscle fibers and gives them shape. This can be a limiting factor for hypertrophy (circumfernce/radial growth) if not addressed.

In general, the body will prefer making the existing fascial system more efficient first, as neurologic comfort, hydration, and alignmnet of fascia are a faster and lower energy means of adaptation. But the theory is that a well designed system will ultimately begin adding new collagen to the system, increasing the dry mass along with the water/gel mass, over long timeframes (months-to-years).

## FASCIAL RECOVERY

Now that we have a basic understanding of fascial nutrition and adaptation, we can start asking questions of recovery. 

Most people have a level of chronic issues/pain. Often this is a combination of neurological protection, adhesions, and as a result, poor movement/joint habits. These can usually be significantly alleviated over a few weeks, and will continue improving for months. 

The recovery curve for plyo/impact is longer than for muscle hypertrophy. A moderate volume lifting session can supercompensate in 48-72 hours; whereas it takes almost that long for the inflammation phase of fascial adaptation to subside; only after which, proliferation can begin. Collagen proliferation and remodeling have a more limited metabolic rate.

Nevertheless, collagen is resilient. It can continue performing at high levels for quite some time, even as it slowly degrades (often at problem areas borne of lingering impingement, stiffness, and unaddressed biomechanical faults. Thus, you need self assessment strategies for determining recovery status.

Generally speaking you have a total fatigue budget which is largely neurologically mediated. The nervous system protects the body from overexertion by signalling perception of fatigue. From one session to the next, you should FEEL recovered and ready to perform. Measured over weeks, you should feel increasingly comfortable, light, and springy as you step into the gym. This is the single best metric you must become familiar with, because there WILL be times that you need to pivot to a recovery routine that day instead of a loaded session, due to fatigue signalling a lack of recovery.

**Random aches and pains**. This is a really good way to determine if your intensity and frequency are optimal. Heavy plyo/impact sessions can leave you feeling a bit like you got hit by a truck. You might develop some random aches and pains after a session. You'll be a bit stiff the next day. These symptoms should last for only about 36 hours, max 72 hours. You might feel like you need to go "stretch out," and it might feel amazing when you do. Random aches and pains might pop up for a day or two, but subside and never return. These are VERY good signs that you're riding the edge of optimal intensity and recovery.

### Dynamic Mobilization
Dynamic mobilization is a major key to accelerated recovery, and must happen the day after a loaded session. The stiffness that lifting and plyo/impact induce, begs to be "stretched out". That tightness is neurological guarding to protect an injury site during the acute phase of inflammation response. However, that tightness begins to work against us when it constricts waste/nutrient turnover.

Stagnation tends to solidify the neurological guarding, and you get caught in a feedback loop. That loop must be interrupted, by gently reminding the nervous system that it has "safe" access to our baseline range of motion, and by forcing waste/nutrient turnover.

This is largely a combination of light trigger point / massage, and dynamic exploration of your end range, gently oscillating in/out of that end range for maybe 60 seconds. There is a "stretch" component here, but again, it's neurological and waste/nutrient turnover related, *not* a long static stretch held for 2-5 minutes.

Additionally, some very light technical work can be done here, but again, no loading. Just flow.

## LOADING TYPES 

The fascia isn't merely a system of ropes and fabric, it's living tissue that responds under various stimuli. Untrained fascia is stuck in sympathetic nervous mode with has brittle, unorganized, short collagen, prone to tearing at even moderate ranges/loads. A healthy system can spike it's immediate capacity to handle a heavy load, or relax into deep elastic stretch. It can expand longitudinally, radially, absorb impact, and provide a spring board for force. Good programming targets all of these modalities.

Previously we looked at nutrient delivery mechanisms. The loads below do offer nutrient delivery; however, their purpose in this context is to cause microtrauma with the goal of supercompensation. These are the things we will do in dedicated loading sessions.

### Ischemic Compression (trigger point)
Trigger point can be performed at an intensity which causes microtrauma to tissues. This is different than using massage that "feels" good as a warmup or recovery. It's real work, requires real recovery, and can leave you sore for the next few days much like working out a new muscle. It pairs well with deep ROM stretching days. Often there is even a stretch component to trigger point work.

### Static Tensile Force
The ability of fascia to maintain stability under load. This can be performing a slow/controlled max effort lift, or a static hold. Of all the modalities, this is probably the least challenging, and most recoverable fascial load. The force/acceleration is lower, and typically not performed at the limits of the tissue capacity. 

### Stretch-Shortening Cycle (SSC)
This is the "spring" response. Efficient fascia has a "crimp" pattern, which literally acts as a spring to preserve and return energy. The muscles flex for static bracing while the fascia bears most of the reflex and recoil. All efficient running/sprinting heavily leverages this mechanism in the foot/calve. Power lifters use it to bounce out of the hole.

Engaging this system is often generally called "plyometric" (plyo) work. It can be engaged for any muscle loaded with a dynamic spring-recoil type movement, and can range from absolute max effort/speed, to light cardio light like jogging, jump rope, footwork, shadow boxing, etc. Max effort movements are heavy on neural fatigue, and produce structural changes to the fascia involved. Light cardio causes little/no microtrauma, and low neural fatigue, but metabolic and conditioning adaptation for steady state capacity. Both need to be trained.

### Impact Resistance
Fascia is a "visco-elastic" system, meaning that it can act as a solid under high acceleration loads, while being pliable to low acceleration loads. Whereas the SSC modality seeks to store and release energy (a spring), impact loading absorbs and dissipates energy. To do this, fascia uses a hydraulic system and the incompressibility of water, with tensile strength, to transduct force along the entire fascial sheet, which distributes load over a larger area. This is perhaps the most overlooked aspect of fascial training in general. Whether by contact sports that overtrain this, or non-contact sports which undertrain it. 

### Deep Range of Motion - Longitudinal ("stretching") 
Previously we talked about "stretching" as a light/moderate dynamic exploration of end range for neurological release. He're we're talking about spending 2-5 minutes in a somewhat static position, with the goal of *active relaxation* to achieve slow lengthening (sometimes called CREEP). Muscles relax, and the load is borne by the fasica, which begin to "uncrimp" (temporarily lose their spring-reflex). This signals fibroblasts and sacomeres to begin proliferation along the lines of force (lengthwise), leaded to permanently longer fibers.

However, stretching a muscle is just one of many adaptations which must take place.
- Rocking in/out of new range to inform the nervous system of new access
- Strengthening and loading in the lengthened position
- Agonist muscle activation

Range that you can control, load, and eventually access purely via agonist activation, is range that becomes permanent.

Crucially, don't mistake deep ROM work for "recovery." Deep stretching forces fluid out of the matrix, calcium ions into the muscle (leading to neuralogical perception of fatigue), and causes slack in that fascia for the next 48-72 hours. There's also potentially a microtrauma aspect to this. Combined with heavy trigger point work, deep ROM days should be regarded as stress load of its own right that requires recuperation.

Without diving into the calcium ion channel reasons, you can actually perform deep ROM work after a hypertrophy or plyo session. If not done soon after, then generally speaking, you should wait 48 hours before engaging in a deep ROM session.

Despite the temporary stress, supercompensation which causes longer fibers and greater ROM, implies *more* capacity for force management, whether muscular, plyometric, or impact distribution.

### Circumferential/radial stretch
This is the classic "pumped stretch for improved hypertrophy." Muscles are contained by the epimysium (external sheath), with perimysium (internal structure) running throughout. This fascial structure functions much like a chinese finger trap. A longitudinal stretch puts a narrowing force on the muscle belly. However, during a pump, the muscles are filled with incompressible fluid. The center can't shrink. So a longitudinal force is translated into a circumferential/radial force; inducing fibroblasts to proliferate radially. Epimysium expansion is necessary for hypertrophy, particularly if muscles tissues have reached the limit of that sheath.

In short, after a lift, while pumped, you should immediately stretch the target muscle group, deeply, to activate this mechanism. Note that very low reps might be counterproductive to achieving a sufficient pump for this to have maximum effectiveness. Furthermore, it might be wise to not maximally load that muscle group again that session, after such a deep stretch, due to microtrauma to the fascial structure.

## PROGRAMMING PRINCIPLES

### Supercompensation and Fatigue Budget
Less is more. Notice we've focused on nutrition, delivery, recovery, and load types; and only now are getting to the actual programming. That's intentional. The hardest part isnt stressing the fascia, it's the discipline to maintain the recovery stack, particularly, adequate spacing between sessions.

To understand the difficulty, we should revist the concept of "fatigue budget." The acute symptoms of an intense session can clear in as little as 24-48 hours. You might feel great, and want to squeeze in an extra session, compress the timeline, or hit your particular sport training hard. However, effective fascial loads cause microtrauma, and proliferation is a hard requirement for supercompensation. On the absolute best recovery stack possible, proliferation might begin 24-48 hours after the session.

The problem is that, if you induce micro-trauma of a similar/same load type after just 2 days (because you feel good), proliferation (supercompensation) is instead replaced with another round of microtrauma and inflammation. If you leave a full week between each unique load type, you're getting 3-5 days of (mostly) uninterrupted proliferation and remodeling.

### Load Variability
You may have noticed that there's quite a variety of load types. It's not particularly easy to achieve full body stress loading of all these types while remaining within the fatigue budget and recovery constraints. Luckily, the specific types of stress being applied in various sessions, are largely orthagonal to one another. Meaning that if we separate sessions by load types, we can stack them closer together and achieve more in a given week.

Because there's so much to cover, each day should be a full body session, combining load types where sensible, and splitting load types across separate days as necessary.

In general these combinations are preferred:
- Hypertrophy + Pumped Stretch (static tensile + circumferential/radial stretching) 
- Plyo (SSC) + Impact
- Deep Trigger Point + Deep Longitudinal stretching
- Dedicated Recovery
- Conditioning / Cardio

### Warmup
Warming up is largely comprised of performing recovery modalities and various loading types, but at sub-microtrauma or tissue modification intensities. A good progression looks something like this:
- Neurogenic tremors for heat and immediate activation of the tissues. Full body.
- Dynamic ROM. No deep stretching, just neural activation/exploration to loosen up into your accessible ROM.
- Moderate trigger point on trouble spots (nothing hardcore).
- Light plyos to activate tension in the muscles before moving into the actual loaded session.

This can be as fast as 20 minutes if you're quick and efficient, and already quite flexible. It could be an hour if you're very thorough with each joint/articulation and micro position exploration. There's a principle at work here. The less accessible your ROM, the more time you need to spend in the warmup, and the less time you should spend heavily loaded. High intensity with poor movement patterns due to inability to reach stable positions, is as likely to injure as it is to supercompensate. Good judgement is required.

Moreover, warmup is not the place for deep tissue work in search of new ROM. It's okay to spend some extra time getting "unglued" if you can feel lingering adhesions/stiffness. Just dont confuse that for deep ROM work, because putting slack into the fascial system immediately before loading (especially plyo/impact) will transfer force into your joints instead of being handled by the fascia. 

After the mobility warmup, you can engage your normal "loading" warmup. So on a lifting day, you might have 1-3 sets of increasing weight until you reach your "working weight." Or on a plyo day, you might do some light bounces and increasing speed/load for 10 minutes until you go into full intensity. 

### Conditioning / Cardio
We add a couple days of light and easy cardio. This should be comfortable, breathing through your nose not your mouth, and ensure that the system is upgrading metabolic/neurological capacity for sustained athleticism. Additionally, the loaded sessions, being full body, can sometimes run long in trying to achieve the full range of exercises. Conditioning is an assist in this regard.

Jump rope, shadow boxing, jumping jacks, active dancing, jogging, light/playful touch sparring. These are the kinds of activities to look for. They should bias continuous lower body flow, with some integrated upper body movements as well (ideally). There is an SSC component, but its not being loaded to max capacity like on plyo days.

Light conditioning is also a good way to add some tension capacity back into the system after a deep ROM day, at low fascial or neural recovery cost. So conditioning can preceed either a hypertrophy session, a plyo/impact session, or both, to no ill effect.

### Frequency, Weekly Plans

Metabolic (muscular) loads recover quickly (24-48 hours), if not done to excess. However, each session also provides a significant and unique fascial and neurological load. This limits sessions to separation by 48-72 hours depending on your recovery status/capacity. Sauna can be added in anywhere in the plan, and can be used for a recovery boost when needed. Below are some ideas for programming frequency.

## INTRA SESSION EXERCISES, STLYLE, AND TACTICS

We can now drill into specific exercises, and how they should be performed for optimal results, depending on the context. 

### Hypertrophy Principles
Mechanical tension is king. Hypertrophy happens because of one thing alone: Mechanical tension on type II muscle fibers. Everything flows downstream from this.

Eliminate the bounce. This is a stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) mechanism, not a hypertrophic one. Instead, pause a moment before coming out of the hole, forcing your muscles to bear the entire load. Relying on the fascial system stretch-recoil response reduces muscle recruitment (mechanical tension) at exactly the most important place for hypertrophy ...

Lengthened position and eccentric loading, has been shown to be the most powerfully hypertrophic part of a rep. To the point that partial reps at the lengthened position can sometimes produce more growth than full reps. Likely a factor why this is true, relates to the circumferential/radial stretch component in the lengthened or stretched position.

No lockout. Resting at the top allows fresh blood flow to come in, feeding type I aerobic muscle fibers which steal some of the mechanical tension from the type II fibers. The internal muscle pressure on a partial rep occludes blood flow somewhat, starving the aerobic fibers from being effective. Usually people have most trouble with the hole, and then some other position as a sticking point farther up. Move just past the sticking point, and then return to the hole.

You might have realized by now that, whatever your 2 rep max is normally, would not be your 2 rep max under such a schema. Rub some soothing balm on your ego, and then reduce the weight. Make sure you can perform enough reps on your last set to have a reasonable pump, so that the radial stretch is effective.


## ODDS AND ENDS

Fascia constitutes 30-50 lbs of mass in an average adult male (including water weight), and it is 25-35% denser than muscle. It's likely that a significant portion of that dense defined look, is a thick and robust fascial system. To the same, it's likely that many hypertrophy plateaus are fascial plateaus. Things like neurological inhibition related to weak fascial support; fascia which lacks longitudinally capacity (too short); or constriction of the epimysium which contains and gives shape to the muscle fiber. 

Exogenous testosterone. Most exogenous steroids are notorious for blunting adaptation and growth of the fascial system. This is no doubt a factor in many of the kinds of injuries we see with steroid users. A notable except to this is anavar, which supposedly activates significant fascial growth.
